How to Compost in an Apartment (No Smell, No Balcony Required)

By The Lighter Footprint Team September 24, 2026 10 min read

Food scraps are roughly 30% of what the average household throws away — the heaviest, smelliest third of your trash. You don't need a yard, a balcony, or a landlord's permission to divert it. Here are four apartment methods ranked honestly by effort, with the no-smell protocol that makes them livable.

Quick pick: least effort → Method 1. Meat and dairy → Method 2. Finished compost for houseplants → Method 3. City already collects scraps → check Method 4 first; it may already be free.

The 4 Apartment Composting Methods, Ranked by Effort

  1. Countertop bin + drop-off — easiest; you collect, someone else composts.
  2. Bokashi bucket — low effort; ferments everything including meat and dairy.
  3. Worm bin (vermicomposting) — highest effort; best finished compost.
  4. Municipal curbside or drop-off program — check first; may already exist where you live.

Method 1: Countertop Bin + Drop-Off (Easiest)

This is composting with training wheels, and there's no shame in it. Keep a small sealed bin (1–1.3 gallons) on the counter, toss in scraps, and empty it once or twice a week at a farmers' market collection point, community garden, or municipal drop-off site.

  • Setup: ~$25 for a bin with a charcoal filter lid, plus ~$8 for compostable liners (or skip liners and rinse the bin — free, slightly grosser).
  • Ongoing: charcoal filters (~$8 for a 6-pack, replaced every 3–6 months) and liners (~$15/year).
  • Effort: 2 minutes a day; 10 minutes a week for the drop-off run. Best for anyone who wants results this week with near-zero learning curve.

How to find a drop-off: search "[your city] food scrap drop-off" — farmers' markets (usually free during market hours) and community gardens are the most common hosts.

Method 2: Bokashi Bucket (Best for Meat/Dairy Scraps)

Bokashi is fermentation, not composting — you layer scraps with inoculated bran in a sealed bucket, and anaerobic bacteria pickle everything over ~2 weeks. The result is a fermented pre-compost you bury in a planter, donate to a community garden, or bag for the trash, where it breaks down far faster than raw scraps.

  • Setup: ~$40–50 for a two-bucket starter kit with bran. Ongoing: bran refills (~$15–20/year).
  • Effort: 5 minutes a day; drain the liquid ("bokashi tea") every few days — diluted 1:100, it's a good houseplant fertilizer.
  • Best for: meat-eaters and dairy lovers. Bokashi is the only apartment method that safely handles meat, bones, dairy, and cooked food without smell or pests.

Honest caveat: the fermented output has to go somewhere — a planter, a garden, a drop-off. With nowhere to bury it, pair bokashi with a drop-off site (Method 1).

Method 3: Worm Bin (Highest Effort, Best Output)

A plastic tote under the sink, a pound of red wiggler worms, shredded newspaper bedding — and within a few months, the richest finished compost of any apartment method. Worm castings are excellent for houseplants, and a healthy bin smells like forest floor, not garbage.

  • Setup: ~$40–60 total — a 10-gallon tote with drilled air holes ($12), a pound of red wigglers ($35–40), and bedding from your recycling bin (free).
  • Ongoing: essentially $0. The worms eat your scraps and multiply.
  • Effort: 10–15 minutes a week — feeding, checking moisture, harvesting castings every 3–4 months.
  • Best for: plant people who want free, premium fertilizer and don't mind a learning curve.

Honest caveats: worms can't handle citrus, onions, or huge amounts of any one thing, and the bin must stay between ~55–77°F. If you travel for 2+ weeks, you need a worm-sitter or a very well-fed bin. This is a hobby, not a set-and-forget system — choose it for the output, not just the diversion.

Method 4 (Check This First): Municipal Collection

Before buying anything: many US cities now offer curbside food-scrap collection or subsidized countertop bins, and some include it in the standard trash fee you're already paying. Search "[your city] organics recycling" or "food scrap collection" — if it exists, it's usually the cheapest and easiest option of all, often $0–5/month.

The No-Smell Protocol

Smell is the #1 reason people quit composting, and it's almost entirely preventable:

  • Freeze your scraps. Keep a container in the freezer and toss scraps in frozen — they don't rot, smell, or attract fruit flies. Transfer to your bin on drop-off day. This one habit solves 90% of smell complaints.
  • Keep the charcoal filter fresh. Bin-lid carbon filters absorb odor for 3–6 months; replace on schedule, because a saturated filter is decoration.
  • Empty weekly in summer, even if the bin isn't full. A half-full bin emptied weekly smells like nothing; a full bin left two weeks smells like regret.
  • Balance moisture. Sour smell means too wet — add shredded newspaper. Fruit flies mean exposed scraps — bury new additions under a layer of browns.

What Goes In / What Stays Out

Always fine: fruit and vegetable scraps, coffee grounds and paper filters, tea bags (staple removed), eggshells, bread, rice, pasta, nutshells, shredded newspaper. Only in bokashi or municipal: meat, fish, bones, dairy, cooking oils, greasy food. Never in a home setup: "compostable" plastics (they need commercial facilities), pet waste, glossy/coated paper, dryer lint from synthetic clothes, anything treated with pesticides.

Costs Compared: Which Method Pays Off

Composting rarely saves money directly — the payoff is waste diverted. The costs are small, and one financial angle is real:

  • Countertop + drop-off: ~$25 setup, ~$20–30/year ongoing. Pays off if your city charges trash by volume and diverting scraps drops you a tier (~$5–10/month saved).
  • Bokashi: ~$45 setup, ~$15–20/year for bran. The free plant fertilizer offsets a $10–15 bottle of liquid fertilizer.
  • Worm bin: ~$50 setup, ~$0/year ongoing. A healthy bin produces several pounds of castings a year — plant people come out ahead.
  • Municipal: $0–5/month. The winner on cost if your city offers it.

Start with the freezer bag tonight — it costs nothing and it's the habit every method depends on. Pick your system this weekend. Your trash bin will be a third lighter within a month.

FAQ

Will composting in my apartment smell?
Not if you freeze scraps until drop-off day, empty weekly, and keep the charcoal filter fresh. Smell comes from scraps rotting at room temperature — the freezer habit eliminates that.
Can I compost meat and dairy in an apartment?
Only with bokashi or a municipal program that accepts them. Don't put meat or dairy in a countertop bin or worm bin — they smell, attract pests, and can harbor pathogens.
What if my building doesn't allow composting?
A sealed countertop bin with frozen scraps is no different from a trash can — it needs no permission. For worm bins, red wigglers in a sealed tote are quieter than a goldfish.
Do I need compostable bags for my bin?
No — a rinse after emptying works fine. If you use them, confirm your drop-off site accepts them; many "compostable" bags only break down in commercial facilities.
